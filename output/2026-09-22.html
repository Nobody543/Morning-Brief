Healey and Burnham Must Learn from Predecessors' Budget Mistakes, Says Institute for Government

Non-partisan policy analysis

The Institute for Government warns that Chancellor John Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham face a deteriorating fiscal position and must avoid the errors of their predecessors. The analysis centers on a core strategic failure: Reeves was constrained by Labour's election pledge not to raise major tax rates, forcing her instead to increase Employer National Insurance Contributions—a move that damaged business confidence and undermined growth commitments.

The IfG argues that Healey and Burnham should use their upcoming budget to present a coherent fiscal strategy combining necessary tax adjustments with a clear vision for economic improvement. They must set a spending envelope compatible with defence commitments and map out a tax strategy explaining how changes promote growth. The analysis notes this is their last pre-election budget for making substantial adjustments without campaign considerations, and warns that chancellors who gamble on improving forecasts have historically failed. The IfG also references the government's earlier handling of prisoner release policy as evidence of choosing short-term political comfort over long-term problem-solving—a pattern it suggests must change at the budget.

German State Elections Show Rising Far-Right Support and Threaten Merz's Coalition

Two state elections on Sunday dealt significant blows to Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU and exposed the fragility of Germany's governing coalition. The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) continued its electoral advance, while the CDU fell below the 5% threshold needed for representation in Mecklenburg West Pomerania—described as a "crass humiliation." Merz framed structural reforms as essential to counter "the authoritarian poison" spreading in German politics.

The result highlights a broader pattern: regular state elections throughout Germany's four-year parliamentary term can destabilise federal coalitions, as parties in government that suffer defeats face pressure from MPs and members to increase their profile at the expense of coalition partners, fuelling internal disagreements. This dynamic is especially risky when coalitions are formed out of electoral necessity rather than ideological alignment.

Lib Dems Reject Equalities Guidance on Transgender Legal Definition Ruling

The Liberal Democrats passed a motion at their annual conference in Brighton rejecting official guidance from the Equality and Human Rights Commission on how organisations should implement a 2025 Supreme Court ruling that defines "woman" in law based on biological sex. The Lib Dems are the first of the UK's three largest parliamentary parties to formally oppose the EHRC guidance, calling it "discriminatory and unworkable." The Greens have previously made similar calls for a different implementation approach.

Victims and relatives affected by the infected blood scandal are having to locate and produce decades-old medical records to prove eligibility for compensation, despite an official inquiry's recommendations on streamlining the process. The administrative burden of assembling old documentation adds to the trauma of reliving the original harm, as illustrated by one claimant whose mother died within nine months of receiving a routine blood transfusion for gallstones.

Awaab's Law Failing to Improve Conditions in England's Social Housing Crisis

Residents living in social housing in England report that Awaab's Law—legislation that came into effect in October 2025 and was designed to force landlords to fix dangerous hazards within 24 hours—is not delivering promised improvements. Tenants describe ongoing battles with mould and damp, and the psychological toll of anxiety and stress-related health effects, despite the existence of the new legal requirement.
