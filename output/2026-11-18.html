Burnham commits to swift implementation of Milburn's wide-ranging youth employment overhaul

Institute for Government analytical/cautionary; The Guardian mixed—editorial support but analytical skepticism on ambition and feasibility

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has promised rapid action on a sweeping report by former health secretary Alan Milburn on young people not in employment, education or training (NEETs). The proposal, described by the Institute for Government as ambitious and well-diagnosed, sets up a new 'Youth Opportunity Service' in every English area by 2029, complete with personalised support plans, expanded apprenticeships, and £1.1bn in employer subsidies. It would replace parts of Universal Credit with a new 'Youth Allowance' and introduce statutory 'Opportunity' and 'Support' guarantees. Milburn, now leading a new Youth Opportunities Unit reporting directly to the Prime Minister, released the findings on Wednesday.

However, the Institute for Government cautions that many proposals rely on legal entitlements without guaranteed funding, risks layering responsibilities on already-stretched services without resources, and leaves unresolved difficult questions on eligibility for disability support that will shape both savings and delivery costs. The Guardian's analysis frames the report as 'almost absurdly ambitious'—going far beyond its original remit to overhaul welfare, health, education and employment systems entirely. Critics also note potential tensions with unions over the report's calls for business tax cuts and regulatory ease to encourage youth hiring.

Milburn's youth welfare reforms risk creating a two-tier system that disadvantages disabled young people

The Guardian opinion/advocacy perspective

The Milburn report's focus on age-based rather than need-based welfare provisions risks creating a two-tier welfare state that could leave young people with disabilities worse off, according to Guardian columnist Frances Ryan. While the plan has positive elements in addressing youth unemployment, it raises significant anxiety for disabled young people about how they will be treated under a system that prioritises age over individual circumstance and need.

Specialist support project helps neurodivergent NEETs enter the workforce

Labour's plan to overhaul welfare support for young unemployed people centres on systemic reform, but the story of Rory Bowman, a 21-year-old with autism, illustrates why specialist help is crucial for those with disabilities. Bowman, part of the near-million-strong NEET cohort, struggled in mainstream education and could not find employment or training that accommodated his neurodivergence. The experience highlights the importance of practical job-learning projects helping such young people transition to work.
