Healey's first Budget: £100 energy support and new taxes on banks and landlords / Institute for Government: Healey's budget defers hard fiscal choices to 2027

Institute for Government analysis is nonpartisan; financial forecasts paint a sobering picture despite modest near-term relief

Chancellor John Healey unveiled his first Budget on 28 October with modest measures targeting household cost-of-living pressures amid what he described as "global economic storms" (the budget snippet identifies the Iran war as driving up oil and gas prices). The centrepiece is a £100 winter payment to 20 million households in council tax bands A-D, extended VAT relief on electricity until July 2027, and removal of gas standing charges. New taxes on banks and landlords fund these measures. Tax thresholds remain frozen for three more years, a decision that will push more people into paying tax or higher rates without raising incomes. The 1% departmental spending cuts will fund a Defence Investment Plan. The Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts that living standards remain under severe pressure, with no quick end to the cost-of-living crisis. Critically, the Institute for Government argues the budget deferred major decisions on defence, welfare, public services and tax until 2027—containing nearly 100 policies but few of significant fiscal impact. By design, Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham avoided tough choices this time, betting that something will change by next year to open political space for welfare cuts or tax rises, though forecasts risk going backwards rather than improving.

The Institute for Government's analysis concludes Healey's budget was "underwhelming" despite containing nearly 100 policies. Despite the scorecard stretching to almost 100 items, only one tax measure (capital allowances changes) costs or saves more than £1bn in the final forecast year—highly unusual. Spending changes were mostly modest or pre-announced; the Defence Investment Plan absorbed most new money, squeezing other services. Most small measures cancelled each other out fiscally. The analyst notes Healey allowed the fiscal buffer his predecessor Rachel Reeves had built to fall, absorbing higher borrowing from increased interest rates. This leaves him only a 56% chance of meeting his main fiscal rule (a current budget surplus by 2029/30), down from 59% at the last budget. Energy support prioritised breadth over targeting: all households receive at least some help, with around 80% receiving the £100 council tax rebate, but richer households in low council tax bands receive the same as poorer ones. The critical weakness: Healey and Burnham delayed decisions on welfare, defence, tax rises and spending cuts until 2027, betting they can change the political environment to enable unpopular choices—or that forecasts improve. However, chancellors hoping for windfalls have historically been disappointed.

Left-leaning critics: Budget offers breathing space but fails to address entrenched inequality

Left-leaning editorial perspective; sympathetic to ambitions but critical of scope and boldness

The Guardian's editorial assessment frames Healey's Budget as "a Rorschach test"—supporters saw dozens of cost-of-living measures as offering "breathing space," critics saw each as modestly valued. The piece argues the budget was more ambitious on how the state can protect the public than how much it spends, taking "a small step in the right direction" in a turbulent world. However, critics argue the measures fall short of the transformative rhetoric from Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who promised to overturn "40 years of neoliberalism." Economist Aditya Chakrabortty argued the budget bore the fingerprints of Martin Lewis, the television financial guru, rather than representing a bold vision from Burnham or careful stewardship from Healey—everyday fixes for household bills cannot meet the turbulent global moment and deeply entrenched economic dysfunction. Political editor John Crace sardonically compared Healey to "Dr Death," noting the chancellor's gloomy demeanour reinforced an already dire economic message despite the 98 measures designed to offer households relief.

OpenAI explores organizational structures as AI enters the workplace

OpenAI has published an essay examining how companies might reorganize as artificial intelligence capabilities advance, presenting three illustrative scenarios for where AI might fit into corporate hierarchies. The essay proposes that AI could be positioned "beneath" existing employees (where each worker effectively becomes a manager overseeing AI assistants, expanding their operation without requiring traditional restructuring), "beside" human workers (as peers in workflow), or "between" layers of management. OpenAI argues the "beneath" scenario—where AI labor grows underneath while existing staff retain positions and gain de facto managerial authority over AI teams—may be an easier adoption pathway than wholesale organizational restructuring, allowing firms to stabilize their evolution from current structures rather than undergo immediate disruption.
