Burnham prepares major conference speech on transforming Britain as election timing remains uncertain

Andy Burnham, now Prime Minister, is set to deliver his first conference speech to Labour, articulating what his inner circle describes as "the biggest change to Britain in 40 years, with a new political and economic model." The speech will pledge action on rough sleeping, social care reform, and youth employment, alongside plans to devolve power from Westminster, reindustrialise the country, and potentially bring energy, water, and housing back into public control.

On the immediate question of election timing, Burnham's team remains divided. Those close to him doubt he will call one soon, believing he can transform the country without a fresh mandate. Burnham himself reportedly sees devolution as his signature achievement rather than early electoral success.

Institute for Government urges reform of incapacity benefits to personalise support and avoid repeating past failures

pro-reform think tank perspective

A new Institute for Government report argues that Burnham's government must overhaul the incapacity benefits system, strained in the post-pandemic period. Spending on incapacity benefits has surged by almost 50% in real terms to £31bn in the latest year, and the proportion of people assessed as eligible has jumped from 47% in 2011 to 80% in 2025. The report warns against repeating the "rushed and crude cuts" that led to Keir Starmer's embarrassing 2025 U-turn on health and disability benefits.

Key recommendations include replacing crude categorical assessments with personalised support plans tailored to individual circumstances, bringing assessments back in-house from private contractors to allow more flexibility, and reforming or abolishing the welfare spending cap that constrains policy options. The thrust is that today's incapacity system focuses too narrowly on determining eligibility rather than helping people move into work.

Council tax system biased against northern England, with London receiving £3bn annual subsidy, thinktank finds

The Resolution Foundation has published analysis showing that the structure of England's council tax system is significantly biased in favour of London and the south-east, with households outside London paying hundreds of pounds annually more than they would under a fairer system. The report quantifies this disparity at £3 billion a year in direct subsidy to Londoners through the current valuation and banding structure. This finding carries direct political weight for Burnham, who has campaigned on regional inequality and promised to devolve resources and decision-making out of Whitehall as a key part of his transformational agenda.

Labour's broad majority fragiles as five distinct progressive voter groups diverge on key policy priorities

Analysis from Survation and 38 Degrees identifying five distinct voter groups within Labour's so-called "progressive majority" reveals deep fractures beneath headline support levels. These voters—spanning idealists, pragmatists, and struggling sceptics—share concerns about cost of living and NHS care but differ significantly over immigration policy, environmental priorities, political trust, and the pace of change needed.

An accompanying MRP poll suggests Labour would retain the largest party status if an election were held now, but would fall well short of an overall majority, with Reform surging into second place. The analysis highlights that Burnham's coalition is more fragile than aggregate polling suggests, with some voter groups drifting toward the Greens and Reform over time.

France leads EU push to exclude UK from 'Made in Europe' policy on green technology and electric vehicles

France is spearheading an effort within the European Union to exclude Britain from the 'Made in Europe' policy framework governing access to European markets for low-carbon technologies and electric vehicles. The policy is designed to curb Chinese competition in green technology sectors, making access to it strategically important for UK manufacturers.

Iran proposes accelerated nuclear talks and Strait of Hormuz reopening in exchange for US sanctions relief

Iran has put forward a proposal to the Trump administration offering to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within six days and begin nuclear negotiations the day after, contingent on the US lifting sanctions on Iranian oil exports, ending all military action on all fronts (including in Lebanon), and releasing frozen Iranian assets. The proposal builds on a memorandum of understanding signed between the US and Iran in June and is designed to expedite nuclear talks well ahead of the original timeline.

Iran's offer reflects economic desperation—the US naval blockade has choked off Iranian oil exports and devastated the economy—yet both sides face the mutual problem that neither wants to surrender leverage first in negotiations. The Strait of Hormuz, through which about 25% of seaborne oil trade normally passes, has been closed since Iran blocked it following US and Israeli airstrikes in February 2026 that killed Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei.

US and Iranian negotiators are actively exploring a phased agreement on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly. Under discussion: Iran would reopen the Strait and Washington would lift its economic blockade. The Strait's strategic importance is acute—roughly 25% of seaborne oil trade normally passes through it, and its closure has triggered record-high fuel prices globally, creating domestic political pressure on Trump before the November midterm elections. According to former US negotiator Dennis Ross, there is roughly a 30% chance of a deal before the midterms.

Rare earths will dominate Trump-Xi summit as China holds key leverage

As President Trump meets with Chinese leader Xi Jinping on September 25, China's control over critical rare earth minerals—essential for defence, automotive, and tech manufacturing—will be a central issue. China controls approximately 70% of global rare earth refining, giving Beijing significant leverage. The US will remain reliant on China for rare earths well into the 2030s despite rapid investment in its own facilities; US output has more than tripled since Trump returned to office but currently meets only 42% of demand.

Some Chinese suppliers have already begun withholding shipments to the US, and a formal export block by Xi could severely disrupt US manufacturing—Ford temporarily shuttered factories in 2025 after rare earth supplies dried up. A trade truce extended to January saw Washington lower tariffs in exchange for eased rare earth restrictions, but the underlying supply imbalance gives Beijing a structural advantage in negotiations.
