Reform UK receives record £36m donation from crypto billionaire Ben Delo

Reform UK has received a £36 million donation from Ben Delo, a British cryptocurrency billionaire, marking the largest single donation ever given to a UK political party. Delo, who relocated back to the UK from Hong Kong, was previously convicted in the US for failing to implement adequate anti-money laundering controls in his crypto operations, though he received a presidential pardon from Trump. The donation far exceeds the previous record of £15 million from Thailand-based Christopher Harborne, also to Reform UK. The scale of the contribution and Delo's background have intensified scrutiny of Reform's financial sources at a time when questions about party funding integrity remain politically sensitive.

MPs reject assisted dying bill in tight Commons vote, likely ending debate for a generation

Parliament's House of Commons voted 286–270 against a private member's bill that would have legalized assisted dying for terminally ill adults with six months or less to live in England and Wales. The measure, sponsored by Labour MP Lauren Edwards, would have required approval from a panel of experts before allowing someone to end their life. The defeat represents a significant setback for campaigners after a previous version was blocked by the House of Lords, which introduced over 1,000 amendments to obstruct it. The government under Andy Burnham did not prioritize the bill, and once this became clear, MPs withdrew support, contributing to its narrow defeat.

Opponents argue the proposed law was "unsafe and unworkable," while supporters expressed devastation that thousands of terminally ill people given hope by the Commons vote last year now saw that progress reversed. Beyond the immediate debate, commentators highlighted a parallel crisis: palliative care in the UK faces severe strain, with nearly one in three people dying without adequate support. The narrow margin suggests the issue remains deeply contested, though the likely political cost of revisiting it means this particular effort may not resurface for years.

Cross-party lawmakers urge PM Burnham to back ban on artificial superintelligence

Over 70 MPs and peers, including 15 former government ministers and former civil service head Robin Butler, have signed a letter urging Prime Minister Andy Burnham to support a legislative ban on the creation of artificial superintelligence (ASI)—technology that would outcompete human capabilities in most domains. The move follows a week of expert warnings about ASI risks, with some researchers assessing a 10% chance it could pose existential dangers. The signatories, drawn from Labour, Conservative, Liberal Democrat, and Scottish National Party benches, have also called for the UK to lead an international effort to prevent ASI development. A bill prohibiting ASI was tabled in the Commons this week. The breadth of cross-party support suggests genuine alarm about the trajectory of advanced AI research, even as questions persist about whether superintelligence remains years away or represents a more distant scenario.

UK imposes sanctions on Israeli settlements, signaling return to 'ethical foreign policy'

left-leaning opinion/analysis

Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband announced the UK's decision to impose economic sanctions on Israeli settlements in the West Bank and revealed he has built an international coalition to support the measure. A Guardian analysis frames this as a return to the "ethical foreign policy" approach pioneered by Labour's Robin Cook nearly 30 years ago, under which the UK would align foreign policy with human rights and democratic values. The piece notes that this move marks a shift from recent UK foreign secretaries, of whom there have been 14 in the past 25 years and seven in just the past six years, few of whom placed progressive ideals at the heart of their actions. The analysis suggests Miliband may finally be a successor to Cook's vision, though the long-term durability of such an approach against competing geopolitical interests remains to be tested.

AI researcher exits Anthropic over extinction risks, reignites AI safety debate

Opinion piece; skeptical but takes the risk seriously

Jacob Coxon, an AI researcher at Anthropic, has publicly resigned, arguing the company is "gambling with our lives" by pursuing self-improving superintelligence—machines capable of building increasingly powerful successors without human oversight. Coxon claimed colleagues remain at the lab not because they disagree about the dangers, but from fear of ceding the field to less cautious competitors, with internal discussion of "endgame" timeframes measured in years rather than decades.

Guardian columnist Gaby Hinsliff examines whether his warnings reflect genuine existential risk or industry hype, acknowledging the difficulty of separating real threat from self-aggrandizing techbro narrative. However, she argues the industry's behavior—with tech whistleblowers regularly warning of potential human extinction—suggests the sector itself is "practically begging to be saved from itself." The resignation adds weight to the cross-party parliamentary effort to regulate superintelligence, suggesting concerns about unchecked AI development span both the research community and elected officials.

Five years after Kabul evacuation, US military still bearing moral weight of Afghanistan withdrawal

In a personal essay, Captain Omer S. Rafiq, who participated in Operation Allies Refuge—the largest noncombatant evacuation in American history during the August 2021 fall of Kabul—reflects on the enduring moral burden carried by those who executed the withdrawal. Rafiq recounts witnessing a grieving Afghan mother hand him her dead child, telling him "You did this," and describes how the moral consequences of failed policies made in Washington fell to 19-year-olds at the gates of the airport.

He argues that American democracy has privatized the grief of national failure by offloading the moral debt onto individual servicemembers rather than the institutions and administrations responsible for the war's conduct and collapse. The force executing the evacuation was extraordinarily young—15 of one rifle platoon were born after September 11, 2001—meaning the war itself was older than they were. Rafiq draws a parallel to the Marines of Beirut, who have lived with the moral weight of institutional failure for 43 years, warning that the Kabul generation faces a similar reckoning unless the locus of responsibility shifts from individuals to the decision-makers who shaped the war's trajectory and collapse.
