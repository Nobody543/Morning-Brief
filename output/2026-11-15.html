Ethics watchdog chief steps back over historical sexual misconduct allegations

Stuart Sterling, chief executive of the newly-formed Ethics and Integrity Commission—established by Labour to ensure probity in government—has stepped back from his role following allegations of inappropriate conduct from his time as a detective at Northamptonshire Police in the early 2000s. Sterling was suspended in 2002 after being accused of misconduct with a female colleague and underwent a disciplinary process resulting in a two-week pay penalty, the maximum possible short of forced resignation at the time; no criminal investigation followed. A second investigation into alleged misconduct in 2008 was inconclusive, and Sterling denies it took place. He was subsequently promoted to detective sergeant, received commendations, and left the force in 2009 to join the Cabinet Office, eventually becoming OBE. The government has said it will "swiftly establish the facts" regarding the renewed allegations.

Milburn's NEET review to propose major welfare overhaul with political risks for Labour

BBC analytical framing

Alan Milburn's long-awaited review of youth joblessness proposes sweeping welfare reforms that will create significant political risk for the government. The review argues young people under 25 should be blocked from sickness and disability benefits, and calls for a dramatic rebalancing of spending: currently around £25 is spent on benefits for every £1 on employment support, and Milburn proposes moving toward a 2-to-1 ratio instead. The underlying claim is that the NEET crisis costs the economy up to £125bn annually in lost output and welfare spending.

Work and Pensions Secretary Andy Burnham will face particular pressure, as past welfare reform attempts have triggered Labour rebellions. Milburn argues this proposal differs because it represents a permanent structural system rather than a passing scheme, emphasizing employment, mental health support, and building a "good life" rather than simply cutting benefits. The BBC's analytical framing notes the proposal's inherent political fraught nature.

Welsh Labour admits party lost touch with voters ahead of post-election review

Ken Skates, Welsh Labour leader since his election in July, presented findings from an independent review by Anand Menon at the Welsh Labour conference on 14 November. The review attributes the party's historic May collapse—falling to third place, losing two-thirds of its vote share and all but nine of its 60 seats, with incumbent First Minister Eluned Morgan losing her own seat—to multiple failures: the party had stopped listening to voters, public services weren't improving quickly enough, and Labour had taken its support for granted. Skates acknowledged these "difficult" findings and vowed to rebuild trust with the electorate. The May 2026 defeat was Welsh Labour's first Senedd election loss since the body's establishment in 1999.

Undercover documentary reveals welfare failures and possible unlawful placement at East Sussex asylum centre

An ITV undercover investigation at Crowborough, a former military base in East Sussex housing asylum seekers, has exposed serious welfare failures and revealed that torture survivors may have been placed there unlawfully. An undercover reporter posing as a welfare officer found inadequate welfare checks and documented problems at the facility, which is run by a firm connected to billionaire asylum camp operator Graham King. The investigation is part of broader scrutiny of the government's strategy to end hotel accommodation for asylum seekers in favour of mass institutional sites. The documentary, "Inside The Asylum Camp," demonstrates that poor screening and inadequate oversight are having serious consequences for vulnerable residents.

Government asylum detention plan criticised over proximity to sewage works

The Home Office plan to build new secure detention facilities at Campsfield House immigration removal centre in Oxfordshire faces environmental objections. According to a written submission from Thames Water, the proposed accommodation blocks would be situated just 20 metres from a sewage pumping station, where detainees should expect insect swarms and strong smells. The plan is part of the government's broader strategy to expand detention capacity for asylum seekers.

Government considers raising age of consent following Russell Brand allegations

The government has indicated it will consider raising the age of consent following allegations made public by Mia Latter, now 37, who waived anonymity to claim she was orally raped by Russell Brand when she was 16 and he was 30. Brand is currently in jail. The Deputy Prime Minister stated that while changing the age of consent "would be a massive step," the proposal "deserves proper consideration and scrutiny." The case highlights a legal scenario—where an adult can lawfully engage in sexual activity with a 16-year-old—that campaigners and some survivors argue should be reconsidered.

Opinion: Britain should veto UAE candidate for UN role over Sudan weapons allegations

Opinion/advocacy column

This is a Guardian opinion column by Steve Bloomfield arguing that Britain should use its veto power to block Lana Nusseibeh, a UAE-backed candidate, from a UN leadership position. The argument rests on documented UN investigations revealing that the UAE has supplied weapons and mercenaries to the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), a Sudanese paramilitary group accused of genocide in Sudan's ongoing war (which began in 2023). Bloomfield contends that Nusseibeh's role has involved "glossing the horror in Sudan" and that her appointment would be inconsistent with the UN's own investigative findings about Emirati involvement. The column notes that Nusseibeh appears to have backing from Russia and the US, but argues Britain has the power and responsibility to block her appointment.
