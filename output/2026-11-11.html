UK Politics

Conservatives pledge £36bn welfare cuts including restrictions on ADHD benefits

Conservative policy announcement; Guardian opinion coverage reflects left-leaning scrutiny

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch launched a "Welfare That Works" policy document on 10 November outlining £36bn in benefit reductions. Shadow minister Helen Whately warned that ADHD claimants with "milder cases" risk being pushed onto "a conveyor belt" toward long-term dependency. The proposal drew immediate criticism from Labour, with the party describing it as "a charter for human misery." Even within conservative media, critics noted that public trust remains fragile on welfare issues and cautioned that the Tories cannot assume the policy will automatically restore confidence in their approach, particularly given recent Labour government struggles with welfare credibility.

UK cabinet secretary calls Whitehall "tame and risk-averse," pledges radical civil service overhaul

Antonia Romeo, the UK's top civil servant, delivered a lecture at the LSE on 11 November describing the civil service as overly "tame, reactive and risk-averse" and pledging a "completely new approach" to managing civil servant performance in the face of AI disruption and global threats. Prime Minister Andy Burnham and Deputy Louise Haigh attended. The Institute for Government analysis welcomed Romeo's rhetoric on the urgency of reform but warned that her credibility depends on delivering "quick, big wins" rather than merely launching yet another review—noting a pattern of failed reform promises stretching back over a decade, with her own review not due to report until summer 2027. Critics cautioned that without concrete early action, belief in the overhaul will fade.

Britain signals willingness to discuss paying into EU funds for electricity market access

Hamish Falconer, the cabinet minister negotiating UK-EU relations, told the Guardian on 10 November that Britain is willing to discuss paying toward EU infrastructure schemes in poorer regions in return for access to the European electricity market. He said the two sides have not yet accepted the principle of linking electricity trade to an EU "cohesion fund" but confirmed the issue will be discussed after the next EU summit as part of deeper bilateral engagement over the coming years.

Labour MPs raise concerns Burnham's 10-year plan lacks radical detail

Labour MPs from the Reindustrialisation Research Group (ReRG)—many representing former "red wall" constituencies that swung Labour's way in the recent election—have expressed alarm that Andy Burnham's 10-year government strategy will not be radical or detailed enough. The group is meeting Sally Jameson, the Cabinet Office minister, on Wednesday to convey their concerns about the prime minister's long-term direction, suggesting tension within Labour's own ranks between Burnham's vision and the expectations of MPs from traditional working-class seats who helped deliver the party back to power.

Niesr warns Bank of England's cautious rate rise risks prolonged inflation

The National Institute of Economic and Social Research (Niesr), a leading independent thinktank, has strongly criticised the Bank of England's response to rising inflation, arguing that last week's hesitant interest rate increase will likely make inflation a persistent feature of the UK economy. Niesr forecasts inflation will remain above the Bank's 2% target until at least 2030, and warns that the timid approach risks locking in higher interest rates for an extended period.

Global Politics

Russia and Ukraine hold first US-mediated peace talks in nine months; discussions focus on security and humanitarian issues

Russian and Ukrainian delegations held trilateral talks with US mediators in Abu Dhabi on 10 November, the first such meeting since February and the first in nine months, lasting approximately four hours. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio described the session as "very productive," while Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called it "constructive and quite substantive," noting discussions centered on humanitarian issues including prisoner exchanges and the return of captured civilians and children. Russian lead negotiator Vladimir Medinsky expressed hope the talks would "advance on the path towards peace." The talks, expected to resume 11 November, will address security guarantees and broader European security arrangements. Trump's envoy Jared Kushner proposed a new European security architecture involving Russia and European states, building on discussions about prisoner swaps and de-escalation. Zelenskiy has expressed satisfaction with messages received from US envoys regarding diplomatic initiatives. Ukraine is meanwhile urging the United States to speed up deliveries of air-defence interceptors as Russia continues targeting the country's power grid, underlining its immediate defensive needs amid ongoing aerial strikes on critical infrastructure.

Ukraine created first-of-its-kind dedicated military branch for unmanned systems to exploit speed advantage

In late 2023, Ukraine's military leadership chose to establish the world's first dedicated armed forces branch for unmanned systems, despite traditional doctrine arguments that drones lack a singular domain of war. The decision reflected a collision between career generals favouring integration into existing branches and civilian-background reformers emphasizing rapid adaptation. Author Oleksiy Honcharuk, who advised the branch's first commander and helped build the 412th "Nemesis" brigade, argues the real innovation is structural: Ukraine's unmanned forces operate in what he calls the "Time Domain"—a competition not for territory but for speed of adaptation. A unit updating tactics and technology every six weeks maintains advantage over one updating annually. With roughly 2.5 percent of Ukraine's military personnel, the unmanned forces have accounted for roughly a third of Russian losses in the past year. Under Russian material superiority across traditional measures, innovation speed was not an advantage for Ukraine but "a condition of existence."

Nvidia board investigates CEO Jensen Huang over alleged Chinese intelligence targeting and affair

Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into founder and CEO Jensen Huang after reports he had a romantic relationship with Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman identified by US intelligence agencies as working for China's Ministry of State Security. Intelligence officials from the US, Singapore, and Taiwan assessed she was a Chinese spy; reports indicate their relationship, dating to at least 2023, may have facilitated at least $7.5 billion in chip shipments through Chinese companies, though Nvidia's board stated it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. Huang admitted to the affair in an email to staff, saying he had "fallen short" of his responsibilities. Congressional scrutiny intensified, with bipartisan calls for answers and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick reportedly receiving classified warnings. Senator Kevin Cramer, a Republican member of the Senate Banking Committee, called for Huang to step down, saying the potential for extortion involving Nvidia's sensitive chips was "so tremendous" that he must resign. The FBI is investigating the matter. Trump, speaking to Huang directly, said the CEO claimed he had no idea the woman was a spy, and Trump said he believed him. Nvidia's stock fell 4% on news of the investigation, raising investor questions about his future as CEO, though Huang pledged to cooperate and the company stated he remains committed to leading Nvidia. Cramer's call for resignation underscores lawmakers' broader concerns about national security vulnerabilities in the semiconductor supply chain and the potential for Chinese intelligence to exploit corporate executives' personal conduct.

Google DeepMind's AlphaProof verifies Signal's encryption with millions of lines of formal proof

Google DeepMind announced a breakthrough in formal software verification: AlphaProof Code agents generated over three million lines of machine-checked proofs in the Lean programming language to verify Signal's core cryptographic protections down to machine instructions. Formal verification—proving mathematically that code behaves exactly as intended—represents a significant advance in ensuring the security of critical systems, moving beyond traditional testing methods by providing absolute assurance rather than probabilistic confidence.

Slovakia's PM Fico accuses Germany's Chancellor Merz of 'blackmail' over Ukraine funding and EU budget

Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico has accused German Chancellor Friedrich Merz of "blackmail" after Merz made Germany's support for the EU's 2028–34 budget conditional on all member states contributing to Ukraine's financing. Merz had written to European Council President António Costa stating that Berlin would not approve the next seven-year budget unless all EU countries agreed to fund Ukraine, which faces a €70 billion funding gap in 2027. The EU is expected to cover approximately €45 billion of that shortfall. Merz is leveraging Germany's position as the largest net contributor to the EU budget to force hesitant member states—including Hungary, Slovakia, Bulgaria, and the Czech Republic—to commit to Ukraine aid. Eastern European leaders, particularly Fico, have rejected linking Ukraine financing to the broader budget negotiations, viewing it as coercive. The dispute reflects a fundamental tension in European decision-making: Merz's use of budgetary leverage to ensure unified support for Ukraine versus the objections of net-beneficiary countries seeking to avoid additional obligations.
