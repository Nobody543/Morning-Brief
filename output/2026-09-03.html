Burnham holds first bilateral as PM with Macron, focusing on Channel migration and EU ties

Prime Minister Andy Burnham used his first bilateral meeting with French President Emmanuel Macron on Thursday to establish closer post-Brexit cooperation on migration, Ukraine, and regional security. The two leaders focused on irregular Channel crossings, where France announced it would deploy an additional 45 riot officers to its western beaches to intercept migrants before launch. The existing £660 million UK-France agreement has prevented over 48,000 attempted crossings, bringing UK numbers to a seven-year low. The meeting also covered Ukraine support, Middle East tensions, and an Anglo-French naval mission in the Strait of Hormuz—signalling alignment on both European security and the emerging geopolitical challenges reshaping the region.

AI wargames show escalation patterns linked to game structure, not model bias

Researchers Dustin League and William Dawson report findings from AI-driven wargames modelling nuclear-armed states in militarized crisis. In initial rounds where AI players set their own objectives, two nuclear-armed nations with a border dispute largely avoided escalation despite tactical nuclear weapons entering play. However, when researchers changed the game structure by explicitly instructing one player to resolve the dispute on its terms, nuclear weapons were deployed extensively. The finding challenges recent arguments that AI escalation behaviour stems from training data skewed toward coercive strategies; League and Dawson suggest instead that escalation is primarily a function of goal structure. They note that existing wargaming literature predicts near-universal nuclear use in such scenarios, but their results suggest off-ramps are available until game objectives themselves are rewritten. The authors call for more rigorous, large-scale analytic wargaming with carefully controlled variables to better understand how AI systems perform under crisis stress before they inform real national security decisions.

Taiwan's military culture remains unchanged despite warnings of need for reform ahead of China contingency

Members-only analysis; limited excerpt available

Michael Hunzeker and Yuster Yu revisit their 2025 argument that major cultural changes in Taiwan's military officer corps and civilian society are essential to competitiveness in a potential conflict with China. A year on, they report seeing no significant shift in military culture—though meaningful organizational reform typically takes years. Their concern is that Taiwan's Lai administration appears not to be seriously addressing the cultural challenge. They cite a concrete example: despite recommending that President Lai reduce the number of general and flag officers (Taiwan has roughly twice as many per servicemember as the US), no meaningful action has been taken. The Ministry of National Defense has a pattern of announcing sudden "transformation" in response to American criticism, suggesting performative rather than substantive change. The analysis flags continued strategic vulnerability in institutional readiness despite acknowledged need for deep organizational reform.
