Brexit back on UK political agenda as voters show appetite for closer EU alignment

A decade after the 2016 referendum, Brexit is re-emerging as a live political issue. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has broken years of political silence on the subject by signalling openness to revisiting Britain's relationship with Europe at the Labour party conference last month. A poll cited in the report suggests around half of UK voters would now consider rejoining the EU, marking a significant shift from the referendum result. The Guardian notes this reverses the "once in a generation" framing former PM David Cameron used to describe the 2016 vote.

All major UK political parties evading the country's fundamental fiscal challenge

According to the Institute for Government, at autumn party conferences across the political spectrum, all major UK parties avoided grappling with the genuine fiscal trade-offs facing the country. The Labour government under Burnham promised reforms to the triple lock (the mechanism that sets state pension increases) tied to new social care commitments, but remained silent on broader tax rises needed for sustainable public services. Opposition parties—Conservatives, Reform, Lib Dems and Greens—each proposed different visions (spending cuts, inheritance tax reductions, income tax rises funded by higher taxes on the wealthy) but none confronted underlying pressures.

The IfG argues that an ageing population and current service strain mean the status quo is unsustainable; without deliberate action, spending and taxes will rise regardless, yet no party is spelling out which services would shrink or which taxes would increase beyond manifesto retail offers. The institute warns this pattern echoes 2024 and risks repeating "fiscal fiction" that has hampered good governance.

UK leaves door open to Chinese EV tariffs as part of EU 'Made in Europe' scheme

No. 10 has signalled openness to imposing tariffs on Chinese-made electric vehicles should the UK join the EU's Made in Europe package (formally the Industrial Accelerator Act). Downing Street said it would "welcome the objective" of UK inclusion after France dropped its opposition to British participation. China has warned such a move would be "discriminatory." The Made in Europe scheme aims to boost European industrial capacity, and Chinese EV tariffs would align with similar EU protectionist measures; UK participation would represent a potential shift toward aligning trade policy with the EU bloc on this issue.

IMF urges Healey to cut pensions and welfare rather than tax banks in budget

The Guardian frames bank-tax supporters' position; IMF position presented as institutional guidance

The IMF's deputy director for Europe has publicly advised Chancellor John Healey to target savings on pensions and welfare instead of raising taxes on banks, directly contradicting reported plans for additional bank levies in the October budget. The position reflects international financial orthodoxy favouring welfare cuts; supporters of the bank tax argue the sector was bailed out by taxpayers during the 2008 financial crisis and can afford higher contributions now. The timing reflects broader budget tensions between fiscal consolidation priorities and sectoral pressure.

Far-right parties surge across Europe with quarter of voters now supporting them

Support for far-right, populist parties has reached unprecedented levels in Europe, with nearly a quarter of voters casting ballots for radical right parties in recent national elections—a fivefold increase since 1995. Trump says US may strike Iran's Pickaxe Mountain nuclear facility

President Trump has indicated the US military may target Pickaxe Mountain, a secretive underground Iranian nuclear facility, in the near term. Pickaxe Mountain (Kuh-e Kolang Gaz La) is a heavily fortified, tunnel-based complex buried roughly 100 metres under a mountain near Natanz in central Iran, approximately 1.6 kilometres south of the Natanz uranium enrichment site that was bombed in US strikes last year. Iran claims the facility is for assembling nuclear centrifuges; analysts suspect it could house uranium enrichment operations or weapons development work. The site has never been inspected by international nuclear watchdogs, and satellite imagery shows ongoing construction and reinforcement of tunnel entrances designed to withstand aerial attack.

Grynspan maintains lead in UN Secretary-General race as voting enters final phase

Costa Rican economist and diplomat Rebeca Grynspan, 70, narrowly leads the race to become the next UN Secretary-General in a sixth straw poll held Friday by the Security Council. The current head of UNCTAD (the UN trade and development body) secured the minimum nine encouraging votes needed to remain viable, though she also received five discouraging votes including two from permanent Security Council members—Russia and China, according to diplomatic sources. Two rivals, Argentina's Rafael Grossi (director of the International Atomic Energy Agency) and Guyana's Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett (former foreign minister), are close behind with eight encouraging votes each. The race is entering its decisive stage with António Guterres's term ending 31 December 2026, though a veto from any of the five permanent Security Council powers could derail any frontrunner. If elected, Grynspan would be the first woman UN Secretary-General in the organisation's history.
