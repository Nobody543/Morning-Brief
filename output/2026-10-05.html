UK Politics

Green Party formally adopts policy equating Zionism with racism; party divisions deepen

Left-leaning coverage emphasizing party divisions and legal challenges

The Green Party voted 1,022 to 813 at its Brighton conference to formally adopt a policy equating Zionism with racism and calling for Israel to be replaced by a single Palestinian state. The motion passed despite strong opposition from most of the party's MPs and is expected to face legal challenge. Zack Polanski, who heads the Greens in England and Wales, missed the vote while campaigning in the Holborn and St Pancras byelection, drawing criticism for a perceived lack of leadership on the divisive issue.

Zionism is a political movement advocating for a Jewish homeland in Israel; the equation with racism is highly contested and reflects deep divisions within the party over Israeli-Palestinian relations. Israel's government condemned the move as a 'stain on history of UK.' Coverage from left-leaning outlets has emphasised the party divisions and legal risks the decision carries.

Kemi Badenoch reframes Conservative 'Nasty Party' label as badge of honour, not liability

Satirical commentary from left-leaning perspective, heavily critical of the approach

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has repositioned the party's long-standing 'Nasty Party' reputation—a label first applied by Theresa May in 2002—as something to embrace rather than overcome. Unlike David Cameron, who moved the party toward the centre after that criticism, Badenoch is framing tough Conservative positions on welfare cuts and strict labour requirements as 'common sense' responses to years in which people 'had it far too good.' The approach marks a deliberate break from recent years of rebranding efforts and represents a more confrontational political strategy that conservatives argue is necessary to address what they see as economic complacency.

Court quashes Home Office decision blocking Gazan families from UK reunion

An immigration court judge has quashed a Home Office decision that blocked mothers and children in Gaza from joining their families in the UK. The judge found the government's 'unsafe journeys' policy was applied unlawfully to two families. The case highlights a practical barrier: families with approved applications to reunite in the UK normally must complete biometric checks (fingerprinting and facial images) at a visa application centre, but the VAC in Gaza has been closed since October 2023, making it nearly impossible for residents to comply with the requirement.

Dodik's separatist allies win Bosnian election as nationalist messaging resounds

Milorad Dodik, the pro-Russia Bosnian Serb nationalist leader, claimed victory for his political allies in Bosnia's October 5 general election. His ally Zeljka Cvijanovic's right-wing Alliance of Independent Social Democrats secured more than 55% of votes with nearly 90% counted, winning one of three seats in the country's ethnically-divided tripartite presidency by roughly 30 percentage points. Dodik, who did not himself appear on the ballot but dominated the campaign with separatist rhetoric, credited Trump's "magnificent victory" in 2024 and Putin's support for the result.

The election outcome underscores the collision between Dodik's push toward Serb autonomy and Western efforts to draw Bosnia toward EU membership. Bosnia's 1992–95 war killed roughly 100,000 people and left the country structured as a weak federation of a Serb-majority entity (Republika Srpska) and a Bosniak-Croat region, supervised by international authorities to prevent renewed conflict. Dodik has long pushed for Serb secession and defied rulings by the international envoy empowered to maintain constitutional order, prompting US sanctions and recent entry bans from Germany and Austria. His election victory, driven partly by economic hardship, signals that separatist nationalist messaging continues to resonate among Bosnian Serbs.
