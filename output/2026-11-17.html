Milburn review calls for 'complete system reset' on education and welfare to get 440,000 young people into work / Nearly One Million Young People Now NEET as Government Eyes Milburn Plan

Alan Milburn's major review of Britain's youth jobs crisis recommends sweeping reforms across education, work and welfare systems. The number of young people not in education, employment or training has surged to almost a million. Milburn, a former Tony Blair-era health secretary, is urging Andy Burnham to invest billions in a "new deal" for young people, including guaranteed jobs support funded partly by reducing benefits. The report frames this as a critical moment for policy, warning of a "downward escalator" trapping young people into welfare dependency. Around a third of young people who persistently skip school in Years 10 and 11 go on to become NEET, the government believes.

Almost a million young people—one in eight—in the UK are not in education, employment or training (NEET), reaching the highest level in a decade. Alan Milburn's landmark review, published today, proposes a comprehensive overhaul to address the crisis. The report recommends a legal guarantee of a route into education, training or work for every young person; a new Youth Opportunity Service; doubling apprenticeship numbers; faster mental health support without waiting for diagnosis; and gradually cutting national insurance paid by employers on workers aged under 25. Milburn estimates the plan would result in roughly 440,000 more young people in sustained work by 2036, and aims to reduce the NEET rate from about 13% to 5%, making the UK one of Europe's best performers on youth participation. The underlying issue is partly demographic: without major intervention, over a million young people could be out of work or education by 2031.

UK Budget introduces world-first ring-fence for preventative spending

Chancellor John Healey's budget introduces a world-first policy: a preventative departmental expenditure limit (PDEL) that will ring-fence spending on projects where initial costs are offset by future savings. Starting with the next spending review, eligible preventative initiatives—such as rough sleeping programmes and early-years support—cannot be raided for immediate needs. The Institute for Government notes this could either transform government by injecting long-termism into public spending, or trigger disputes over what qualifies as preventative. The policy applies a blanket approach across all departments, unlike other nations that protect only specific sectors like public health; nearly any spending could theoretically be argued preventative, making eligibility determination a significant challenge.
